1. Executive summary / TL;DR

The verdict. kb is already 80% of the way to being an excellent e-ink reading library, because its core content unit — a self-contained, inline-CSS, JS-optional HTML artifact served verbatim at a stable per-artifact URL — is exactly the payload e-ink browsers render best. What's missing is the delivery layer: kb has no EPUB export, no OPDS catalog, no e-ink CSS theme, and no on-device reading client (verified: no real EPUB/OPDS support in crates/, web/, docs/ — the only matches are incidental substrings like FakePublish and base64 hashes in package-lock.json). The single biggest, lowest-risk win is a server-rendered "e-ink reading mode" (a JS-free, high-contrast, paginatable view + a static browse index) that works on every device including frozen-WebKit Kindles/Kobos. The single best experience comes from pairing kb with KOReader via an OPDS feed of on-the-fly EPUBs, which gives native pagination, dictionary, and font control on cheap hardware. The single best device for the stated goal — reading a lot of text, genuinely better than a monitor/phone — is a monochrome 300-PPI e-reader running KOReader (jailbroken Kindle/Kobo, or a Kobo Clara BW), reached over Tailscale or a path-bypassed OPDS endpoint.

The honest caveats

(a) The "e-ink is scientifically better for your eyes" claim is weaker than marketing suggests — the cleanest head-to-head study (Siegenthaler 2012) found no eye-strain difference vs LCD, the two most pro-e-ink biological studies (Yuan 2021, Wang 2023) had E Ink Corporation co-authors, and blue-light-filtering glasses are refuted by the 2023 Cochrane review[V-eyes]. E-ink's defensible wins are sunlight legibility, no PWM flicker (with a DC-dimmed frontlight), not emitting strong light at bedtime by default, and single-purpose focus — not a proven comprehension or eye-health boost. (b) Color and fast-refresh remain mutually exclusive in shipping hardware (mid-2026), and kb's design-rich artifacts (syntax highlighting, charts) lose information on grayscale or 150-PPI color panels — kb's AA-locked visual floor (commit 9d65a5a) is a partial hedge — it locks contrast, not hue-collapse (it has no monochrome/desaturation hooks yet; see §6). (c) kb's React SPA is a poor e-ink fit everywhere and unusable on frozen-WebKit readers; do not route e-ink reading through it.

2. The reading-quality thesis: why/when e-ink beats a monitor or phone

The core motivation is reading a lot of text on something better than a monitor or phone. The evidence supports that goal — but the reasons it's true are not the ones marketing pushes, so it's worth being precise, because the precise reasons drive the device and architecture picks below.

Where e-ink genuinely wins (well-supported)

  • Sunlight / glare — unambiguous, physics-based. E-ink reflects ambient light like paper, so it becomes more legible as light increases; OLED/LCD fight the sun and lose. Indoor phones run ~300–500 nits; sunlight readability needs ~2,500+ nits and even then glare washes emissive screens out[ergonomics-6]. For outdoor or bright-room reading, e-ink wins decisively.
  • No PWM flicker — but contingent on the frontlight. Many OLED phones (notably Samsung at ~240 Hz PWM) flicker by switching the emitter; ~10–15% of people perceive this as strain/headache, worst at low brightness[ergonomics-7]. E-ink's panel is bistable — the image holds with zero power and zero refresh, so there is no refresh flicker. But the frontlight is a separate matter: cheaper frontlit e-readers can use PWM dimming, so the flicker-free benefit is not guaranteed by e-ink per se — it depends on the reader using DC dimming (quality readers such as Onyx Boox do)[ergonomics-7][V-eyes]. Honest framing: a bistable panel with a DC-dimmed (or off) frontlight sidesteps PWM entirely; a cheap PWM-frontlit reader on low brightness does not.
  • Bedtime / sleep — real, but reframed. Chang et al. (2014/2015, PNAS) showed a backlit iPad before bed delayed the circadian melatonin rhythm by >1 hour, suppressed melatonin, lengthened sleep onset, and cut REM vs a printed book[ergonomics-4][ergonomics-5]. Crucially, this indicts emissive backlit screens, not e-ink specifically — the study had no e-ink arm, ran the iPad at max brightness, and confounded device with room lighting[V-eyes]. The defensible takeaway: a reflective e-ink reader with the front-light off emits essentially nothing, and even a warm amber front-light is a fraction of a phone's output — so e-ink is materially better than any backlit phone/LCD at night by default. But a frontlit e-reader on full cool brightness is not automatically "sleep-safe."
  • Single-purpose ergonomics. Hard to RCT, but real: a dedicated reader has no notifications, tabs, or app-switching, removing the attentional cost that degrades sustained reading on a phone/laptop. Battery measured in weeks (Kindle Paperwhite ~10–12 weeks light use) versus hours reinforces an uninterrupted reading posture[ergonomics-8].

Where the case is weaker than claimed (be honest)

  • Eye strain vs LCD — not proven. The most direct within-subjects study, Siegenthaler et al. (2012, Ophthalmic & Physiological Optics), found reading on LCD vs e-ink is "very similar in terms of both subjective and objective measures… it is not the technology itself, but rather the image quality that seems crucial"[V-eyes]. That last clause is the key reinforcing point: the differentiator is image quality (contrast and PPI), which is precisely why this report's thesis is "buy a mono 300-PPI panel" — sharpness and contrast, not the e-ink chemistry, are what help. Benedetto et al. (2013, PLOS ONE) did find an LCD fatigue penalty — but n=12 and it compared a tablet (Kindle Fire HD) to a dedicated e-reader, confounding device class with panel type[ergonomics-1][V-eyes]. The two most pro-e-ink biological studies (Yuan 2021, Wang 2023) had E Ink Corporation staff as co-authors[V-eyes]. Net: evidence suggests eye-comfort benefit; it does not prove one. Digital eye strain is dominated by blink/accommodation/ergonomics, not panel tech — an e-ink device read at the same close distance for hours drives the same problems[V-eyes].
  • Comprehension — modest and genre-dependent. The "screen inferiority effect" (Delgado et al. 2018 meta-analysis, 54 studies, g ≈ −0.21) shows paper beats screens for comprehension — but it concentrates in expository/time-pressured text, vanishes for narrative reading, and was measured against LCD/scrolling devices, not e-ink[ergonomics-9][V-eyes]. "E-ink reads like paper for comprehension" is plausible (it's paginated, reflective, paper-like) but not established.
  • Blue-light glasses — refuted. The 2023 Cochrane review (17 RCTs, >600 participants) found blue-light-filtering lenses "probably make no difference to eye strain, eye health or sleep quality"[V-eyes]. Don't lean on blue-light framing.

Where e-ink loses outright

Slow refresh and ghosting make it poor for anything dynamic — periodic full-screen flashes are needed to clear residue[ergonomics-6]. Color e-ink (Kaleido 3) is a downgrade for text: the color-filter layer makes the screen darker/grainier, halves color resolution to 150 PPI (still 300 PPI mono), and worsens ghosting[ergonomics-6][color-1]. Note that 300-PPI sharpness, while genuinely good, is parity with print and good LCD — a modern 400+ PPI phone is sharper; sharpness is not an e-ink-specific advantage, just an enabler of the image-quality that the Siegenthaler finding flags as what actually matters. For pure long-form text, monochrome 300-PPI is the right tool; color is only worth it for comics/magazines.

Thesis verdict

Buy e-ink for fatigue-in-bright-light, no-flicker (DC-dimmed), low-night-emission, and focus — and read mostly monochrome prose. That is precisely the content profile of kb's Markdown notes and prose-heavy research artifacts. It is not the profile of kb's interactive, color-coded, chart-heavy artifacts — the central tension this report keeps returning to.

3. Hardware spectrum, by tier

The market fractures along two axes: how locked the software is (closed appliance → open Android) and what the panel does (mono Carta → CFA/pigment color → fast RLCD)[hardware]. The load-bearing question for kb is binary: does the device run full Android with a modern Chromium, or a frozen vendor WebKit? Everything else is a usability tax layered on top of that gate[browsers].

Tier 1 — Basic locked e-readers (reading appliances)

Sealed firmware, built only to read. No real browser, no third-party apps.

  • Kindle Paperwhite 12th gen (2024): 7″ Carta 1300 mono, 1264×1680, 300 ppi; 16 GB; ~12-week battery; IPX8; warm frontlight; $159.99 (Signature $199.99)[hardware-1][hardware-4]. Fastest Kindle.
  • Kobo Clara BW: 6″ Carta 1300, 1448×1072, 300 ppi; 16 GB; $129.99; iFixit-repairable — the B&W value pick[hardware-1][hardware-6].
  • Kobo Clara Colour: same chassis, 6″ Kaleido 3 (300 ppi mono / 150 ppi color, 4,096 colors — see Tier 4); $149.99 — cheapest big-brand color reader[hardware-1].
  • PocketBook Era/Verse: broadest format support (EPUB/PDF/MOBI/CBR/CBZ), TTS, no forced account.

Browser? Effectively no. The Kindle "Experimental Browser" is WebKit (KHTML), not Blink. Importantly, a silent Nov-2023 update (firmware v5.16.4) made the Kindle browser materially more capable on 10th-gen+ models — it now renders "HTML5, JavaScript, CSS, WebP," handles much of the modern static/mobile web, and no longer OOMs on complex sites[V-kindle-browser]. But its own first-run warning still says it's "intended for use with simple websites… Sites with animation or other complex features may load poorly," there are no tabs, no SSE, and a modern React SPA remains the explicit anti-use-case[V-kindle-browser][browsers-3]. Kobo's beta browser saw no comparable leap — "very simple, can't do much," good for captive portals and Wikipedia[V-kindle-browser].

How kb best reaches it: the device browser can't reach a self-hosted server meaningfully. The realistic paths are (C) EPUB via Send-to-Kindle email — the only push channel for a stock, un-jailbroken Kindle besides the experimental browser — or (C) OPDS after jailbreaking to run KOReader (the WinterBreak jailbreak, Jan 2025, covers essentially all Kindles from the 5th gen / 2013 onward on the firmware shipping at the time, and installs KOReader — but Amazon patches specific firmware versions over time, with successor exploits (WinterBreak2/AdBreak) for newer builds, so treat "any firmware" as point-in-time, not permanent[network-jailbreak]). For stock Kobo, the vendor sync-URL trick (below) can pull a self-hosted library with no KOReader at all[network-2].

Tier 2 — Large-format note e-readers (closed/curated)

A4/A5 writing slabs; excellent pen, deliberately minimal software — not general tablets.

  • reMarkable Paper Pro: 11.8″ Gallery 3 color (~229 ppi), amber frontlight; 64 GB; $629 (risen from $579); closed Codex OS, weak reading app[hardware-1][hardware-5]. reMarkable 2: 10.3″ mono, $399.
  • Supernote A5 X2 "Manta": 10.7″ mono, 1920×2560, 300 ppi, no frontlight; Android-11-based; uniquely allows APK side-loading; user-replaceable battery + mainboard; $459[hardware-2].
  • Kindle Scribe (2024): 10.2″ mono, included pen; ~$400–500; locked. Its Active Canvas lets you write inside reflowable books, but you cannot export a sideloaded doc with annotations baked in — most closed of the four, no API[annotation-4].
  • Kobo Elipsa 2E / Sage: 10.3″/8″ mono, stylus, native Libby borrowing.

Browser? reMarkable/Scribe: none at all (reMarkable's "web app" is a file-management portal, not a browser)[browsers-8]. Supernote: a PDF-hyperlink-only mini-browser.

How kb best reaches it: these need a PDF/EPUB side-load pipeline (kb → EPUB → reMarkable Cloud API or Send-to-Scribe). For reMarkable specifically, the cleanest transport is SSH-over-USB: rM 1/2 ship with root SSH enabled out of the box (password in Settings), so you can push files (and run Toltec/Entware → Tailscale) without the now-paid, "sluggish" reMarkable Connect cloud subscription — SSH-USB is free and faster[network][annotation-reMarkable]. (Caveat: the Paper Pro and newer firmware lock SSH down — don't assume it survives updates.) reMarkable also has the best programmatic story for annotation extraction (typed text via rmscene/MCP, OCR for handwriting). Supernote A5 X2's APK side-loading means KOReader can run on it — the most open of the note tier.

Tier 3 — Open Android e-ink (run anything)

The defining trait: full Android + Google Play, so any browser (Chrome/Firefox/EinkBro) and any app (incl. Tailscale, KOReader APK) installs. This is the tier that does what a normal tablet does.

  • Onyx Boox Note Air4 C: 10.3″ Kaleido 3; octa-core, 6 GB/64 GB; Android 13 (not 15 — corrected); Wi-Fi 6; included pen; $499[hardware-3][V-boox]. Note Air5 C (2026): Android 15, Snapdragon 690, ~$530, 3-yr support[V-boox].
  • Boox Note Max: A4 13.3″ Carta 1300 mono, 300 ppi, octa-core, Android 13, included pen; ~$650–689 — the sharpest large monochrome substrate; "marking up blueprints or musical scores is a dream"[hardware-3][color-4].
  • Boox Tab Ultra C Pro / Tab X: 10.3″/13.3″ productivity-class (Android 12).
  • Boox Go 10.3 Gen 2 (Android 15) / Go Color 7 Gen 2 (Android 13): lighter Android readers with NeoReader[V-boox].

Browser? Yes — full Chromium/Firefox. A modern React SPA renders correctly here — the only tier where kb's SPA runs live[browsers]. Caveats are experiential, not capability: ghosting, no smooth scroll, manual refresh-mode switching (Normal/Regal vs A2/Speed), mid-tier SoCs, and on-screen-keyboard lag. Two corrections from verification: (1) Android version is fragmented (12→13→15) and Onyx never upgrades the OS major version post-purchase; (2) Play Integrity / certification friction is real — first-run requires registering the GSF ID, and the Note Air4 C has documented integrity-check failures that can break banking/DRM apps[V-boox]. The killer operational issue is aggressive Wi-Fi-after-sleep power management — the tunnel and any live connection drop the moment the screen sleeps, so treat Boox as sync-then-read-offline, not a live terminal[network-5].

How kb best reaches it: anything works. Cleanest = (A) point the browser at the raw artifact URL or (B) the e-ink mode; best round-trip annotation = live SPA comment UI.

Tier 4 — Color e-ink (two competing chemistries)

The buying decision is Kaleido 3 vs Gallery 3[color][hardware-4]:

  • Kaleido 3 (CFA): RGB filter over a mono panel; 4,096 colors, 150 ppi color / 300 ppi mono; fast (UI/video-capable); slightly tinted/darker background; visible filter grain. Best for reading with color accents. The mainstream choice (Kindle Colorsoft, Kobo Libra/Clara Colour, most Boox, Palma 2 Pro).
  • Gallery 3 (ACeP, CMYW pigment): true pigment per pixel; platform max ~50,000 colors (shipping devices realize ~20,000 via dithering), 300 ppi; refresh 0.35 s B&W → 1.5 s best color; no true black ("dark indigo"), weaker text contrast. Best for drawing/comics/static images. Flagship: reMarkable Paper Pro.
  • Spectra 6: signage only (~6 colors, ~20 s full refresh) — irrelevant for reading.

2025–2026 correction: Gallery 3 has effectively failed in the consumer market — E Ink's roadmap dropped it and only a handful of devices shipped it. The live color line remains Kaleido 3 (Kaleido 4 was announced/anticipated — ~200 ppi color, better blacks — but had not reached any shipping consumer device as of mid-2026), with Spectra 6 pivoted to signage[V-color]. Treat "Gallery 3 vs Kaleido 3" as a slightly stale framing; Kaleido 3 is what you'll actually buy.

How kb best reaches it: color e-ink renders kb's color artifacts technically but at grainy half-resolution. kb's AA-locked visual floor (commit 9d65a5a) helps with contrast but does not solve hue-collapse — it has no monochrome/desaturation hooks yet (§6 typography), so color-coded artifacts still lose their signal on grayscale; the real fix is the proposed e-ink theme, not the current floor. For pure text, color is a net negative; prefer mono.

Tier 5 — Fast-refresh / RLCD (the "smooth" outliers)

  • Daylight DC-1: NOT e-ink — a 10.5″ reflective monochrome RLCD ("LivePaper"), 1600×1200, ~190 ppi, hardware-capable 6–120 Hz, software-capped at 60 Hz; Android 13; amber PWM-free backlight; $729 (not $799 — that was a pre-launch figure)[V-daylight][hardware-7]. Scrolling/video are LCD-smooth with no ghosting — uniquely good for reading long scrolling docs. But: not bistable (tablet-like battery, not weeks), only 190 ppi (vs 300 on a base Kindle, "not sharp enough" per a long-term reviewer), weaker contrast than an old Paperwhite, and monochrome only — a year-long developer report flagged losing syntax-highlighting as the killer for code[V-daylight][color-9].
  • Modos Paper Monitor / Flow: true e-paper driven by an open-source Caster FPGA controller, up to 75 Hz (record for real e-paper); HDMI/USB-C external monitor + the upcoming color/stylus Modos Flow (13.3″, ~$619 mono/$719 color, Crowd Supply, ~Dec 2026 delivery — crowdfunding figures that may slip)[V-daylight][color-8]. A hacker tool today; the only roadmap device promising both fast refresh and color — the architecturally correct answer for kb's rich artifacts, but unshipped.

How kb best reaches it: Daylight runs full Android → (A)/(B) in Chromium, smooth. Modos is a monitor → it just shows your PC's browser, so kb's SPA or e-ink mode both work but the "device" is your computer.

Tier 6 — Phone-sized e-ink

  • Boox Palma 2: 6.13″ Carta 1200 mono, 300 ppi; Android 13 + Play; no cellular; $249.99[hardware-9][V-boox]. Palma 2 Pro: 6.13″ Kaleido 3, Snapdragon 750G, Android 15, 5G data only (no calls/SMS), ~$399[V-boox].
  • Hisense A9: a true 6.1″ mono e-ink smartphone (calls + SMS); Snapdragon 662, Android 11; ~$659 global[hardware-10].

How kb best reaches it: full Android → anything. The Palma 2 is the pocket "Readwise-on-e-ink" form factor; kb's raw artifact URLs read well on it.

This is the e-ink-friendly path.

Self-contained artifacts. The authoring contract (docs/authoring-artifacts.md) forbids external resources: inline CSS, real <title> + <h1>/<h2> hierarchy, stable section ids, no <base href>/target="_top"/window.parent.*. On-disk sizes are tiny: canon 11–20 KB, research artifacts ~20–60 KB — an ideal e-ink payload[serve]. Markdown artifacts (invariant 16) render server-side to clean, JS-free HTML (markdown::render_page) — the closest thing kb has to an EPUB-ready chapter today[export][readux].

Export / share. Two bundle exports exist: kb share (curated, always strips <template id="kb-prompt"> via scrub_export, resolves links, copies relative assets, does not inject the daemon's /_kb/* scripts) and kb download --folder (raw, unscrubbed zip — would leak generation prompts; never build an export from it naively)[export]. kb index-page generates a self-contained static HTML index of a kb (group-by status/category) — the closest existing artifact to an e-ink browse page[readux].

Search, reading progress, comments. Hybrid BM25+vector (GET /api/search, RRF k=60; mode=keyword/--offline skips the embedder). Reading-progress (invariants 8/19), scroll resume, TOC heatmap, sessions, atlas/gallery, and kb-comments (invariants 6/18) — all are 100% client-JS + cross-origin postMessage + SSE, owned by the live React SPA[readux].

Constraints an e-ink device imposes (and what survives)

  • Slow refresh / ghosting. Smooth scroll, transitions, backdrop-filter, sticky elements, and the virtualized React gallery all ghost or smear[readux][typography][color]. What survives: static, paint-once artifact HTML; kb share static export; kb index-page.
  • JS / SSE limits. kb's reading-progress, scroll resume, comments, atlas, and the live gallery all need JS in both the iframe and the parent SPA, plus a held-open EventSource (web/src/api/sse.ts). On frozen-WebKit readers the SPA won't boot at all (no fetch, no Flexbox/Grid, ES5 only)[browsers][readux]. The reading-progress feature is therefore dead on e-ink — but degrades silently (no errors, the page just renders).
  • Typing pain. Search is typing-first; e-ink keyboards make it the bottleneck — which is why a browse-first, link-only surface matters more than a search box[readux][network].
  • Auth friction. kb binds 127.0.0.1:4000 by default → not reachable from any other device out of the box[auth]. The non-loopback gate (middleware.rs::is_loopback_origin, invariants 3/4) fails CLOSED and can't be header-spoofed, so an e-reader always needs a bearer token or a proxy that injects one. The SPA's fetch() cannot attach Authorization itself, so a bare LAN-bind 401s every API call — only a proxy-injected bearer (or a VPN-via-loopback bypass) makes the SPA work[auth]. On prod kb.example.com, the heavy Authelia login SPA is the single biggest friction point on a weak e-ink browser (slow render, ghosting on the password field, cookie-eviction login loops); TOTP is off (a mercy), and remember_me: 1M helps[auth].

5. Usage models / integration architectures (A–E)

Five architectures, scored against the hardware split. The recommended path is to layer them, not pick one.

(A) Point the device browser at kb's URLs as-is

How it works. Open http://<id>.artifacts.<domain>/ (raw artifact, e-ink-friendly) or — only on capable hardware — the SPA. Relies on kb's existing daemon.

Fit. Raw artifact URL: works on anything that can render static HTML (incl. post-2023 Kindle for simple artifacts). The SPA: Boox/Daylight/full-Android only — it will not boot on frozen WebKit[browsers].

kb-side work. None to function; ideally an e-ink CSS layer (§6).

Pros. Zero pipeline; live content; on Boox the SPA's search/comments/atlas all work. Cons. SPA motion fights e-ink; auth friction (bearer/Authelia); SSE battery cost; useless on the largest installed base. Reading quality: raw-artifact path = good everywhere it renders; SPA path = mediocre even on high-end Boox.

(B) kb "e-ink reading mode" — server-rendered static theme, no SPA JS

How it works. A new daemon route (e.g. /eink/<id> or ?eink=1) serves the parsed artifact body in a minimal server-rendered shell: semantic markup, system fonts, pure black-on-white high-contrast CSS, server-side prev/next pagination, and a static link-only browse index built on the already-existing e-ink-perfect JSON endpoints (…/docs, …/tags, …/folders, …/docs/by-path/{*path})[readux]. Search becomes a plain <form GET> → server-rendered results over the existing hybrid index. This route must strip kb-prompt exactly as the share/scrub path already does (invariants 5/9).

The hard part — how the body is split into pages. "Server-side prev/next pagination" is the single hardest engineering question in this phase, so name the mechanism rather than hand-wave it. Options, weakest-to-strongest[typography]:

  • CSS multicol (column-width with a fixed viewport height) — simplest, no JS, but fragile: it splits figures/tables awkwardly and gives no reliable page count. Acceptable as a degrade-to path.
  • Paged.js or a Readium-style paginator — gives real @page, running heads, and counter(page) numbering, but @page margin-boxes still have poor native-browser support in 2024–2026, so don't rely on margin-box headers in a device browser, and it needs JS (rules it out on frozen-WebKit).
  • Server-side splitter (recommended, universal) — split the body at top-level <h2>/section boundaries (or an estimated words-per-page budget) into N static HTML pages with prev/next links keyed on a ?page= param. The only approach that paginates on frozen-WebKit readers (no client JS). Correction (per code review): this is not a reuse of parser::extract — that retains only flattened BM25 text (no body DOM, and it does not surface section ids). The splitter must be a fresh lol_html rewriter pass over the source bytes read from disk (mirroring the existing serve path in routes/artifact.rs), generating slugs or reading the artifact's authored stable ids itself.
  • Degrade to scroll when none of the above fits (e.g. a single un-sectioned artifact) — one tall static flow.

The practical stance (revised per code review): ship the JS-free black-on-white themed page with degrade-to-scroll FIRST — it delivers ~80% of B's value with none of the splitter risk — then add the server-side splitter (the genuinely hard, higher-effort piece) once the theme is proven.

Fit. Everything — including stock Kindle/Kobo experimental browsers, because it degrades to static HTML with JS off (the recommended Kindle config; Good e-Reader measured a 70% performance gain from disabling JS[browsers-1]).

kb-side work. Moderate for the theme + browse index (kb index-page is the seed) + a <form GET> search; but the pagination splitter is a hidden High (a new HTML-rewriter pass, section-id generation the parser doesn't provide, table/figure break handling), and a live serve-time theme needs a net-new inline-<style> injector (today the daemon injects only <script>, not CSS).

Pros. Universal coverage; tiny payload; no motion ghosting; page-turn model suits e-ink; reuses kb's source-serve path + search index. Cons. No offline; typing on Kindle/Kobo still painful; loses SPA richness. Reading quality: good on all devices — the single most universal improvement per unit effort.

(C) kb export → EPUB + OPDS catalog feed

How it works. kb export --epub converts each artifact (or a collection) to reflowable EPUB3; kb serves an OPDS 1.2 acquisition feed (Atom/XML — the stable, universally-supported version; OPDS 2.0 JSON is optional polish) ordered newest-first (KOReader's sync requires "sorted by new")[serving][koreader]. KOReader, Calibre-Web, Thorium, and any OPDS client browse and download. The conversion path is well-trodden: kb artifacts are structurally close to EPUB XHTML chapters (semantic headings → nav/ToC, stable ids → fragment anchors, inline CSS, kb-tags/kb-category → OPF metadata)[export][epub].

The hard part — and the honest limits. kb has zero EPUB/OPDS support today (verified — the only matches across crates/, web/, docs/ are incidental substrings like FakePublish and base64 hashes in package-lock.json, not real references)[export]. Five blockers to clean conversion:

  1. Embedded JavaScript — the #1 blocker. Hand-authored artifacts routinely ship interactive JS, and JS will not reliably run in the e-ink EPUB targets that matter. Verification refined this: Apple Books and Thorium/Readium do execute EPUB JS, and Kobo's mobile apps do — but Kobo e-ink hardware, Kindle (EPUB→KFX strips scripting), and KOReader (CREngine, no JS) do not[V-epub-js]. So for the e-ink targets, assume JS won't run and flatten dynamic content to static SVG/PNG before conversion. The concrete mechanism — not "pre-render" hand-waved — is a headless-browser render of the artifact: load the self-contained HTML in headless Chromium, let its JS execute, then capture the resulting computed SVG (or rasterize a canvas/widget to PNG) and inline that static result in place of the script + its target node[epub]. This is the single highest-leverage conversion step.
  2. HTML5 not XHTML — needs a tidy pass (void-element closing, attribute quoting).
  3. No package scaffolding — kb generates no .opf/nav/spine/cover.
  4. CDN subresources dangle (the share asset-walk only copies document-relative assets).
  5. Source from kb share's scrubbed output, never the raw download.

Recommended pipeline: headless-render JS→inline static SVG/PNG, strip remaining JS + kb-prompt, HTML→XHTML tidy, pandoc -t epub3 (most conformant, good SVG handling via the inline-svg class) or Calibre for fidelity, validate with EPUBCheck, declare UTF-8 explicitly (the Send-to-Kindle landmine — undeclared UTF-8 is assumed ISO-8859-1 and mangled)[epub]. Restrict the first cut to Markdown + JS-free HTML; flag JS-bearing artifacts as not-convertible[export].

Fit. The widest reading-experience tier — true native pagination, dictionary, font/margin control on cheap KOReader hardware (Kindle/Kobo/PocketBook/Android). The only option that gives native e-reader rendering on the lowest tier.

kb-side work. High and not cleanly bounded — the headless-Chromium dependency is a heavy new runtime (kb has zero browser deps today) and EPUB determinism/validation is a long tail; it's "bounded" only if scoped to JS-free/Markdown artifacts with no headless render. Correction (per code review): do not route this through the ShareHostKind/ShareBackend enum — those backends upload to a remote host (Cloudflare/GitHub Pages) and their result/teardown shape is all about remote objects, so a local EPUB builder + OPDS feed is a category mismatch. The clean seam is a new kb_core::epub module that consumes kb share's already-scrubbed StagedShare.files (reusing the share engine's scrub/stage front-half) plus a read-only routes/opds.rs daemon route[export].

Pros. Best raw reading quality on the most devices; offline once downloaded; standards-based; future-proof. Cons. Snapshot, not live; progress/comments live in the reader app unless paired with (D); complex artifacts degrade. Reading quality: best-in-class for prose.

(D) KOReader integration — Wallabag-shim (cheap) or a custom kb.koplugin (rich)

This architecture has two variants, cheapest first.

(D-shim) Implement a Wallabag-compatible read endpoint — no KOReader code at all. KOReader ships a built-in Wallabag plugin that authenticates to a self-hosted server with Authorization: Bearer, downloads newest items as per-article EPUBs, runs an offline upload queue, and does two-way archive/delete. If kb exposes a Wallabag-compatible API shim (the /oauth/v2/token + /api/entries shape, returning kb artifacts as EPUBs), the existing built-in Wallabag plugin pulls kb content with zero custom KOReader code[serving][koreader]. The grounding material ranks this as the single biggest reach-per-effort channel after OPDS itself — it rides clients (KOReader and phone Wallabag apps) that are already shipped and maintained. The cost: kb must emulate enough of the Wallabag entry/annotation API surface, and credentials are stored on-device.

(D-plugin) A bespoke kb.koplugin (Lua: LuaSocket + LuaSec + rapidjson) calls kb's JSON API for search, downloads an artifact (EPUB via C, or HTML rendered by KOReader's CRE/MuPDF engine), opens it, and on close posts reading progress back to kb. The Wallabag plugin is the near-exact precedent to clone. This is the richest path (live kb search on-device) but the most maintenance.

Phasing within D: zero-code OPDS first (days, part of C) → Wallabag-shim (reuses a shipped client, days–1 week) → kb.koplugin (1–2 weeks) → a kosync-style progress endpoint to make kb the progress server.

Fit. KOReader-capable devices = essentially every e-ink device (Kindle jailbroken, Kobo/PocketBook/reMarkable native, Boox/Android via APK; latest KOReader v2026.03 "Snowflake" even added Boox Note Air5 C support)[koreader][V-koreader].

Hard constraints (both variants): (a) KOReader's HTTP stack cannot do proxy/CONNECT tunneling — kb must be reachable directly with a token the daemon accepts (the kb-bot client_credentials path or a LAN/loopback instance), not via interactive Authelia[koreader][auth]. (b) KOSync is progress-only and content-blind (MD5 doc-hash matching) — kb must supply its own IDs (see the progress-identity sketch in §6). (c) Correction: KOReader has no built-in web browser — the "browser" is a third-party text-mode plugin (omer-faruq/webbrowser.koplugin); don't design around a built-in one[V-koreader]. (d) The third-party opds_plus.koplugin (cover grids, per-catalog sync, sync-folder selection) is proof that OPDS is "low-effort, zero-custom-code" — strengthening the "OPDS/shim first, bespoke plugin last" ordering[koreader].

Pros. Closes the loop — kb's search and reading-progress become first-class on-device; offline + sync-back; reuses proven patterns. Cons. D-plugin is the most engineering; KOReader-only; annotation write-back is a known gap. Reading quality: best overall — native EPUB rendering + live kb search/progress.

(E) Sync-offline bundle (kb share static export) sideloaded periodically

How it works. kb share already produces a kb-prompt-stripped static export (invariant 9). Periodically rsync/USB/Syncthing it to the device; read via the device browser (Android) or, if exported as EPUBs (C), via the native reader. A variant worth calling out: drop the exported EPUBs into a Calibre-Web-Automated (CWA) watch folder — CWA then inherits the EPUBs into its library and gives you its mature, already-shipped KOReader progress-sync, native Kobo shelf-sync, and Kindle-EPUB-Fixer for free, rather than kb reimplementing any of them[market][serving]. That makes (E)+CWA a concrete low-effort route to native Kobo/KOReader sync without building (D). Cron/Syncthing refreshes the bundle.

Fit. All devices. kb-side work. Minimal — kb share exists; add an EPUB-bundle mode (overlaps C) + an index landing page (overlaps B).

Pros. Fully offline, lowest runtime complexity, no live daemon dependency, privacy-preserving — directly answers the Pocket/Omnivore data-deletion failures[market]. With the CWA watch-folder variant, you also get native sync for free. Cons. Stale between syncs; no progress/comment write-back (unless via CWA); needs an index page to navigate hundreds of files. Reading quality: equal to B (HTML) or C (EPUB) minus liveness.

6. Cross-cutting concerns

Networking & auth (LAN / Tailscale / Authelia / bearer)

kb's security is one binary gate: loopback vs not[auth]. Three exposure options, best-to-worst for an e-reader:

  1. Tailscale / VPN (recommended). Put both hosts on a tailnet; tailscale serve --bg 4000 → identity-gated TLS, stable URL, SSE works. The wildcard catch: MagicDNS historically gave one hostname per machine; opt-in wildcard subdomain resolution landed in early-2026 (~v1.96), but wildcard TLS certs remain unsupported — so HTTPS <id>.artifacts.<host> iframe routing still doesn't work over a tailnet, and the framed gallery view still breaks. The escape hatch is the right primitive anyway: GET /api/kb/{kb}/artifact/{id} (artifact_bytes, docs.rs:759) returns one clean artifact same-origin over the apex host. Because tailscale serve proxies from 127.0.0.1, a bare-metal daemon bound to host loopback sees a loopback peer and bypasses bearer auth — access then rests entirely on the tailnet (don't use Funnel for private corpora). Correction (per code review): the prod kb.example.com daemon is containerized and published as 127.0.0.1:4000:4000, so requests arrive from the Docker bridge gateway, not loopback — the bypass does not apply there; you still need a proxy-injected bearer (or must add the gateway IP to trusted_proxies). For an e-reader-over-Tailscale setup, run a bare-metal loopback daemon for that corpus, or inject the bearer at the proxy.[auth]. On full-Android Boox, install the Tailscale APK; but it won't auto-start on boot and Wi-Fi-after-sleep kills the tunnel — sync-then-offline[network-1][network-5]. KOReader/Kindle/Kobo firmware can't run a VPN client — the network must come to them (LAN or public HTTPS); the one exception is reMarkable 1/2, where root SSH + Toltec can install Tailscale (tailscale up --ssh) over userspace networking[network].
  2. Reverse proxy injecting the bearer (prod pattern). Traefik's kb-inject-bearer middleware replaces Authorization with the daemon token after the edge auth runs — the only browser-viable path for the SPA[auth]. On kb.example.com both routers chain authelia@docker,kb-inject-bearer (artifact subdomains are gated too — prod contradicts self-host.md's "auth-free" claim). The friction is the one-time Authelia SPA login + cookie persistence on a weak browser[auth].
  3. Bare LAN-bind + bearer. addr = "0.0.0.0:PORT"; works for curl/scripts but the SPA 401s every API call (can't attach the header) — discouraged by the hardening checklist[auth]. (For stock Kobo with no KOReader at all, the vendor sync-URL trick — pasting a self-hosted sync URL into Kobo account settings, BookOrbit-style — is a separate path that pulls a library + covers + progress natively; CWA's Kobo-native shelf-sync is the polished form of this[network-2][market].)

For OPDS/KOReader specifically, the clean answer is the standard recommendation: bypass forward-auth on the OPDS/API path, then protect that path with Basic Auth. Configure Authelia/Authentik to bypass forward-auth on /opds (and the artifact path), and let the backend's own HTTP Basic Auth guard it — KOReader speaks Basic only (no digest, no interactive SSO), so a single user/pass field then works directly[network-9][network-koreader]. A CIDR/tailnet bypass is the alternative when the reader is usually on home Wi-Fi or behind Tailscale. Authelia's OAuth2 bearer (authelia.bearer.authz, PAR+PKCE) works for a custom client (the kb-bot path) but no off-the-shelf OPDS reader speaks it[network-9][auth].

Offline vs live

Across every device class, flaky Wi-Fi + aggressive sleep make download-then-read-offline win decisively[network]. KOReader OPDS download pulls the full file; afterward it reads with Wi-Fi off. Do not design around OPDS-PSE page-streaming — it is image-only (CBZ), not EPUB, so streaming is not an option for kb's prose EPUBs; download is mandatory[network]. Prefer manual progress push/pull — auto-sync can clobber newer local progress on a stale pull; books match by MD5 content hash, so download the same file on each device[network]. This argues for (C)+(E) as the durable core and (D) for the sync loop, with live SPA (A) reserved for Boox-on-home-Wi-Fi.

Progress identity (the kosync mismatch)

KOSync is content-blind: it keys documents by a 32-char MD5 doc-hash and stores only furthest progress, no filename or content[koreader]. kb's history lifecycle (invariants 8/19) is path/id-based. Correction (per code review): truly byte-stable EPUB output is not realistically achievable — the recommended pipeline's headless-Chromium render is non-deterministic, and the EPUB/ZIP container embeds timestamps (ZIP entry mtimes, OPF dcterms:modified; pandoc stamps a fresh modified-date per run). So make the persisted hash→id map the primary mechanism: record each MD5 ↔ artifact-id the first time KOReader reports a hash, and reconcile from there. If you also want stable hashes, force a reproducible-zip writer (fixed mtimes, pin dcterms:modified to the source mtime) and restrict the hashed subset to JS-free/Markdown artifacts (no headless render). Either way, the kosync endpoint must be a NEW route that mints a synthetic visit via history_record_open before writing progress — kb's /history/reading POST requires a server-issued visit_id and 404s on any it didn't mint (invariants 8/19), so a bare MD5+percentage can't write to it directly.

Typography & an e-ink reading theme

kb's visual floor (docs/authoring-artifacts.md §317–378) is AA-locked and reduced-motion-aware (commit 9d65a5a) — a strong start that already half-solves e-ink. But it has no @media print, no monochrome, no prefers-contrast, no forced-colors hooks anywhere, leans on mid-gray --muted/--rule/--surface tokens that ghost and muddy on 16-level panels, uses off-white/cream backgrounds (legacy canon worse: --panel #ebe1ce on --bg #f4ede2 → two near-identical grays after hue is discarded), and encodes signal in blue accents (the floor uses hex, not OKLCH — that's the SPA) that map to indistinct gray[css]. An e-ink theme (deliverable today as author-pasteable media blocks or a kb share/export-time stylesheet; a live serve-time CSS layer would need a new inline-<style> injector — the daemon currently injects only <script>, not CSS) must:

  • Force pure #000 on #fff; collapse the gray ramp to a few well-separated steps; replace hairline --rule/--surface fills with solid ~1px black or whitespace (kills ghosting)[css][typography].
  • De-color every signal: links → always-underline; semantic --warn/--danger/--ok and .dot/.stat → patterns/weight/icons/labels, never hue; --accent → #000[css].
  • Kill all motion (transition, scroll-behavior:smooth, backdrop-filter, animations) — treat e-ink as prefers-reduced-motion: reduce unconditionally[css][typography].
  • Break the fixed-height two-pane shells (height:100vh; overflow:hidden) into one paginatable flow; avoid position:sticky; add break-inside: avoid on callouts/figures/tables[css].
  • Type: bias a screen-optimized sans with large x-height (Verdana/Arial/humanist) but a robust serif (Georgia) is fine at 300 ppi — decide by x-height/stroke uniformity, not the serif/sans label; bump body weight (~450/500) to counter grayscale-AA thinning; force -webkit-/-moz-osx-font-smoothing: grayscale (subpixel AA color-fringes on mono panels); max-width: 60–66ch; line-height: 1.5 set on body and inherited (per-element line-height breaks Kobo's user setting; Kindle ignores <1.2); use % not em for horizontal margins[typography].
  • Default dark mode OFF — inversion turns the whole panel into a large dark region that maximally ghosts; let the device do its own (better-tuned) inversion, and use currentColor for borders/SVG so they track night mode[typography].
  • Reconcile the dual token vocabularies — alias the legacy canon names onto the floor names at serve time. Canon uses --text/--border/--panel/--link; the floor uses --fg/--rule/--surface/--accent. The actionable fix (not just "redefine both"): in the e-ink serve-time layer, set the floor tokens to the e-ink values and add alias declarations so the legacy names resolve to the same values — --text: var(--fg); --border: var(--rule); --panel: var(--surface); --link: var(--accent); — so a single e-ink stylesheet themes both vocabularies without touching artifact source. (The durable fix is to unify token names in the authoring contract, but aliasing is what unblocks a serve-time theme today.) This is the single biggest portability gotcha[css].

A reusable starting stylesheet and a production gate ("render → desaturate to grayscale → confirm every distinction still reads at ~16 levels") are spelled out in the typography material[typography]. (This very report applies a slimmed-down version of that gate: see its @media (monochrome), (prefers-contrast:more), (forced-colors:active) and @media print blocks.)

Color / large-format / fast-refresh for rich artifacts

No shipping device wins on all axes (mid-2026)[color]. The fork: color information load-bearing → reMarkable Paper Pro (Gallery 3) for static viewing, accepting slow paint + weak black; sharp + smooth-scrolling code/docs, mono OK → Daylight DC-1 (60 Hz RLCD, but kills syntax color) or Boox Note Max (13.3″ 300-ppi mono, BSR). Watch, don't buy: Modos Flow (open color e-paper at 75 Hz) — the only roadmap device promising both. kb's AA-locked visual floor is only a partial hedge — it guarantees contrast, but it has no grayscale/monochrome adaptation yet (§6 typography), so the real fix for monochrome devices is the e-ink theme proposed there, not the existing floor.

Annotation two-way sync

kb-comments anchor on text (not coordinates), which is exactly right for reflowable artifacts[annotation]. Paths ranked: (1) Boox via the live SPA — round-trip with no new code, but it is the full SPA + sandboxed iframe + SSE + ?cm=on annotator stack, so it inherits every caveat §4 applies to architecture A (ghosting, SSE battery cost, Wi-Fi-after-sleep dropping the connection, auth friction): "zero plumbing," not "e-ink-pleasant". (2) KOReader — export highlights as JSON (built-in Exporter), a small adapter maps text+note onto a kb comments CLI call; one-directional, batch, fully scriptable. (3) reMarkable — typed annotations extract cleanly via rmscene/MCP; handwriting needs OCR; use SSH-over-USB to avoid the paid Connect subscription. (4) Readwise bridge (any device) — highlight → Readwise → poll GET /api/v2/export → translate to kb comments; decouples kb from every vendor but adds a paid dependency and carries only highlight-text. (5) Kindle Scribe / Supernote — closed; on Kindle, sideloaded-doc highlights live only in My Clippings.txt (store books additionally cap at ~10% clipping), so flow-back is email-only and not realistic[annotation]. Anchor on text, treat handwriting as OCR-best-effort.

7. Precedents & the gap kb uniquely fills

The "personal library → e-ink" space splits into two camps that rarely overlap[market]:

  • Ebook managers — Calibre-Web-Automated (the strongest precedent: watch-folder ingest, EPUB/KEPUB conversion, OPDS, native Kobo sync, built-in KOReader progress-sync), KOReader, Kavita, BookLore. They nail EPUB/PDF delivery but search is metadata/filter-only — no full-text, no semantic search, and no library-wide full-text search even in KOReader (an explicitly tracked gap; a user with 5,000 EPUBs can't search across them)[market].
  • Read-it-later — Readwise Reader (best e-ink story: runs on any Play-Store e-ink device with a four-toggle E-ink Mode, ingests heterogeneous content) but is cloud-only, ~$8–10/mo, no offline, no semantic retrieval, no addressable HTML artifact; Instapaper/Matter are cloud SaaS. The surviving self-hostable read-it-later tools are Wallabag (open-source; its plugin/API is the basis for the (D-shim) channel above) and Karakeep (ex-Hoarder, 24k+ stars) — both name-checked as the post-shutdown survivors. The graveyard is the argument for kb: Pocket shut down 8 Jul 2025 (data purged 12 Nov 2025), Omnivore went dark 15 Nov 2024 — two flagship products vaporized in 13 months, taking libraries with them[market].
The unoccupied position kb holds

A self-hosted, search-first server for LLM-generated HTML artifacts with BM25 + bge-small hybrid search. No precedent combines all four of: (1) HTML as a first-class self-contained served document (not extracted article text), (2) library-wide hybrid semantic search (every precedent tops out at metadata filters, per-document search, or cloud keyword search), (3) self-hosted file-on-disk durability, (4) LLM-artifact-native authoring contract[market]. The gap kb must close for parity is the e-ink delivery story — and the field has standardized the answer: OPDS + KOReader progress-sync (CWA's exact recipe). An OPDS endpoint serving on-the-fly EPUB/HTML + a KOReader-compatible sync server (or simply dropping EPUBs into a CWA watch folder to inherit its sync stack) marries the one capability the whole field lacks (semantic search) to the one delivery channel it has standardized on.

8. Concrete recommendations

Phased path

Phase 0 — Today, zero code (validate the thesis). On a Boox (borrow/buy a Palma 2, $249.99), open https://<id>.artifacts.kb.example.com/ directly, or over Tailscale hit …/api/kb/<kb>/artifact/<id>. Confirm the raw-artifact reading experience is what you want before building anything. This already works.

Phase 1 — e-ink reading mode (B) + browse index. The biggest quality lift per unit effort and the only universal one. A ?eink=1 / /eink/<id> server-rendered route with the §6 black-on-white, paginatable, motion-free theme; the server-side section/word-budget pagination splitter (§5-B), degrade-to-scroll fallback; strip kb-prompt (invariants 5/9); a kb index-page-derived live link-only browse surface over …/docs/…/folders/…/tags/…/docs/by-path; search as a <form GET>. Works on stock Kindle/Kobo, Boox, Daylight — everything. Also add monochrome/prefers-contrast/forced-colors/print hooks to the visual floor so future artifacts self-adapt, and alias the legacy token names onto the floor names (§6) so the e-ink layer themes both vocabularies.

Phase 2 — EPUB export + OPDS feed (C). kb export --epub (source from kb share's scrubbed StagedShare.files; headless-render JS→inline static SVG/PNG (§5-C); Markdown + JS-free HTML first, flag the rest; HTML→XHTML tidy; pandoc -t epub3; EPUBCheck + explicit UTF-8) and an OPDS 1.2 acquisition feed sorted newest-first with auth path-bypass → Basic Auth. This unlocks native pagination/dictionary on every KOReader device and full offline reading, plus two free downstream channels: Send-to-Kindle email of the rendered EPUBs (the only push path for a stock un-jailbroken Kindle; mind the 50 MB email / 200 MB web caps and the UTF-8 landmine) and dropping EPUBs into a CWA watch folder for native Kobo/KOReader sync. Optionally expose the EPUB feed as an RSS/Atom feed of newest artifacts too — that feeds KOReader's News downloader, rsspub, and Calibre Fetch News with no extra device code[serving].

Phase 3 — KOReader (D) + kosync-style progress. Start with the Wallabag-compatible shim so the built-in Wallabag plugin pulls kb artifacts with zero KOReader code; graduate to a bespoke kb.koplugin (clone the Wallabag pattern; LuaSettings for base URL + a directly-accepted token, no Authelia; "Search kb" → results → download → showReader) only if live on-device search is wanted. Add a kosync-style progress endpoint — a new route that mints its own history visit before writing progress (it can't POST to /history/reading as-is; §6) — using the persisted hash→id map (§6) to report reading position back to kb's history. Closes the search + progress loop on-device.

Annotations ride along: Boox uses the live SPA comment UI now (Phase 0); a KOReader-JSON → kb comments adapter lands with Phase 3.

The single best pick for the stated goal

Goal: read a lot of text, genuinely better than a monitor/phone — i.e. e-ink. That goal is mono prose, where e-ink's real differentiators (sunlight legibility, no PWM flicker with a DC-dimmed frontlight, low night emission, single-purpose focus) all land and its real weaknesses (color, refresh) don't matter. (Note 300-PPI sharpness is parity with print/good LCD, not an e-ink-exclusive edge — a 400+ PPI phone is sharper — so it's an enabler of the image-quality the evidence credits, not a differentiator in its own right.)

  • Device: a monochrome 300-PPI e-reader running KOReader. Concretely, a Kobo Clara BW ($130 — KOReader installs without a jailbreak, via the KFMon/NickelMenu launcher path; keep it on firmware 4.x, since NickelMenu's menu needs <5.0) for pocket reading, or a jailbroken Kindle Paperwhite 12th-gen if already owned. If a larger canvas for code/wide artifacts is wanted and color is dispensable, the Boox Note Max (13.3″ mono). Avoid color e-ink for this goal — it's a text downgrade.
  • Architecture: (C) EPUB + OPDS, consumed by KOReader, reached over Tailscale (or a path-bypassed OPDS endpoint on kb.example.com protected by Basic Auth), read offline, with (D) progress sync back to kb. This gives native pagination + dictionary + font control (best reading quality), works on the cheapest hardware, keeps content offline against flaky Wi-Fi, and — uniquely versus every precedent — carries kb's hybrid search into the catalog.
  • Interim until C/D ship: Phase-1 e-ink mode (B) read in KOReader's (third-party) web plugin or a Boox browser, or just the raw …/artifact/{id} URL (A) over Tailscale. All three are usable today or near-today.

If instead you want one device that also does everything a tablet does (live SPA, search, comments, the occasional color artifact), the answer flips to a Boox Note Air5 C or Palma 2 over Tailscale with the SPA — accepting a laggier, ghosting-prone experience and the Wi-Fi-sleep caveat. But that's not the stated goal; the stated goal is reading a lot of text, and for that the mono-KOReader-EPUB path is the recommendation.

Open questions / risks

  1. EPUB conversion fidelity for interactive artifacts. The corpus's value is often the embedded JS, which dies in every e-ink EPUB reader. How much of the library is prose/Markdown (convertible) vs interactive (not)? The export must flag, not silently mangle. Risk: building EPUB export and finding half the corpus is non-convertible — even with headless-render-to-SVG, genuinely interactive widgets flatten to a single static frame.
  2. Auth model for KOReader. KOReader can't do interactive SSO or proxy CONNECT. Committing to OPDS means committing to a path-bypassed Basic Auth or a directly-accepted bearer on a dedicated endpoint — a deliberate weakening of the Authelia-everywhere prod posture. Acceptable on a tailnet; think before exposing publicly.
  3. Progress identity mismatch. KOSync is content-blind (MD5 hash); kb's history lifecycle (invariants 8/19) is path/id-based. The §6 sketch (deterministic per-artifact-id EPUB → stable MD5 → id mapping table) is the proposed bridge, but it's fragile to any change that perturbs exported bytes.
  4. Eye-comfort expectations. The evidence does not guarantee an eye-strain or comprehension boost — only sunlight/flicker/night/focus wins, and even the flicker win is contingent on a DC-dimmed frontlight. Calibrate expectations: the upgrade is real but for different reasons than the marketing claims[V-eyes].
  5. Color/fast-refresh roadmap bet. Modos Flow (open color e-paper, 75 Hz) is the architecturally correct device for kb's rich artifacts but is unshipped crowdfunding (delivery ~Dec 2026, may slip) — don't build for it yet; the grayscale floor is the safe hedge.
  6. Maintenance surface. A KOReader plugin (or Wallabag shim) + an EPUB pipeline + an OPDS feed are new long-lived surfaces against fast-moving targets (KOReader monthly CalVer; EPUB readers; Boox firmware). Start with B (no new client, all in-daemon), and prefer the Wallabag-shim and CWA-watch-folder routes over bespoke code precisely to minimize that surface until the value is proven.
