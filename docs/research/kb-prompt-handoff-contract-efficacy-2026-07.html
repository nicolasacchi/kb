Does the kb-prompt handoff contract actually work?

Every kb artifact is supposed to carry an execution contract "written for a stranger who will never see the chat." In this corpus's whole history, no stranger had ever been handed one to check. So I ran the test: a fleet census, then a blinded experiment that hands separate agents different slices of an artifact and measures how well the work transmits. The contract is real, adopted, and useful — and two of its load-bearing justifications do not survive contact with evidence.

method: census (23 corpora, 1,704 files) + blinded 3-condition transmission (7 artifacts × 3 slices, 6 blind judges, Opus) + 2 adversarial agents · lab /tmp/kb-prompt-efficacy-lab · no code changed · all proposals triage-gated

The premise being tested

kb's authoring guide is emphatic: each artifact embeds an inline <template id="kb-prompt">, capped at 8 KB, holding a machine-readable execution contract — "the handoff, not a TL;DR." It is structurally validated, composite-scored, protected by architecture invariant #5, and stripped on public export. An enormous amount of ceremony rests on one untested assumption: that the contract, handed to a fresh agent, actually conveys the work.

Six prior Fable-frontier runs interrogated the memory subsystem and determinism. None touched the authoring convention itself. That is the frontier here — not a review of the code that implements the template, but an empirical test of whether the artifact it produces does its job.

Part 1 — Census: is it even adopted, and in what form?

A deterministic scan of every *.html across all 23 locally-mounted corpora (the 21 kb.toml corpora, plus the two nested facs mounts): 1,704 files, of which 697 carry the template.

Two things stand out. First, adoption is real but partial — roughly half of authored artifacts carry the template; whole corpora skip it entirely (one corpus 0/7, one repo's infra half 0/11 while its sibling app half is 11/11 — one two-repo kb with one compliant half). Nobody ever exceeds the byte cap.

Second, and more telling: the structured execution-contract form the guide describes is vanishingly rare — about a dozen files in the entire fleet (8 in research, 2 in kb-docs, 1 in another project). The other ~686 kb-prompts are freeform: a paragraph of intent, a mission summary, a note on provenance. The canonical placeholder marker ("Replace with the filled-in scaffold") appears in zero files — authors either fill the template or delete it to a stub, never ship the skeleton. So in practice the kb-prompt is a short freeform provenance note, and the "ROLE / PRECONDITIONS / STEPS / POSTCONDITIONS" execution contract is an ideal that ~1.6% of templates actually adopt.

Part 2 — Transmission: hand a stranger one slice, measure the work that survives

To measure transmission rather than assert it, I split 7 artifacts into three disjoint slices and gave each to a separate plan-only agent that could read the repository's code but not its docs/. Each produced an INTENT / PLAN / GAPS write-up. A blinded judge then read the ground-truth artifact and scored the three anonymized plans.

The three conditions

  • prompt — the <template id="kb-prompt"> inner text only (472 B–4.5 KB).
  • body — the visible artifact text with the template removed (9–30 KB).
  • floor — three lines: title, category, tags (<300 B). This is roughly what a search result or gallery card shows.

The v024 milestone plan — the single richest, most contract-like prompt (7.3 KB of ROLE/STEPS) — was excluded by the separation filter because its prompt and body deliberately share verbatim command chunks. Note that limitation now; it returns below.

The ranking was body > prompt > floor in all six artifacts — perfectly consistent (I recomputed every per-artifact triple from the raw judge output; they reconcile exactly). Two clean signals emerge, and one tempting third signal that turns out to be an illusion.

Signal 1 — standsThe kb-prompt carries a real handoff. A freeform kb-prompt plus repository access let a stranger recover the work's decisions well enough to execute in 6/6 cases; title-plus-tags managed 1/6. The judges' reasons for the floor's failure are concrete and code-independent: without the prompt or body, teams "would build the corpus-changelog verb the doc explicitly rejects," inferred a "wrong-by-inference 'greenfield' status," or "would produce a different artifact than the actual work item." That is a large gap, and code access is held constant across both arms, so the comparison is fair.
Signal 2 — stands (bounded)Thin context costs completeness, not honesty. Groundedness stayed high in every condition, floor included (9.0/10): the failure mode of a starved agent was an honest GAPS list, not fabrication. Agents knew what they didn't know. (Caveat: groundedness here was cross-checkable against already-shipped features, and floor did double the fabrication rate — 0.33 vs 0.17 — so "untouched honesty" would overclaim; call it "far less degraded than completeness.")

Part 3 — The claim that died: "the kb-prompt is insurance for when the body is stripped"

The seductive third reading of the table: the body beats the prompt whenever both are available (ranked #1 in 6/6), so the kb-prompt is marginally redundant — its real payoff must be the scenario where the body is gone and the prompt is the survivor. Invariant #5 even names such a scenario: the outbound scrub. I wrote that framing down as the headline design takeaway. The adversarial pass killed it on the one fact it was told to check.

Inverted linchpin (code-verified)The scrub does the opposite of what the insurance framing assumes. kb_core::scrub::scrub and scrub_export remove the <template id="kb-prompt"> element and keep the body (scrub.rs:80–124). kb share static export always strips the prompt (scrub_export); the live serve path strips it only on a non-loopback request and only when an operator opts a kb in — strip_kb_prompt defaults to false (config.rs:530, :1704). So the "body-stripped, prompt-survives" scenario does not exist: the scrub deletes the prompt and preserves the body, and by default deletes nothing at all.

With the linchpin inverted, the redundancy claim collapses on three further counts the experiment's own design forces:

  • No body+prompt arm exists. The three slices are disjoint by construction (the template is removed from the body; the separation filter enforces zero chunk overlap). The study measured body-alone vs prompt-alone — which cannot establish that the prompt adds nothing given the body.
  • The margin is inside the bias envelope. The "blinded" judge was blind to the plan label but never to the condition — every floor subject announced its own slice ("the handoff is a 3-line metadata stub"), and the judges' justifications name each slice ("Received the document body"). A ~1.5-point intent-fidelity edge and a perfect 6/6 ranking are exactly what a "the body saw more input, so rank it higher" demand characteristic produces on its own. (The prompt >> floor gap, at ~3.5 points with concrete missing content, is far too large to be an artifact of this bias — which is why Signal 1 survives and the redundancy claim does not.)
  • Intent-fidelity against the body is circular. The ground truth the judge scores against is the body (~90% of the artifact's words). A slice that is the body will trivially match it better than a slice that is a compression of it.

So "the body strictly dominates the prompt" is refuted, and "the prompt is redundant while the body is present" was never tested. What remains is only Signal 1 restated: the prompt beats metadata, not that it loses to the body.

Where the kb-prompt is genuinely the only carrier

If not the public scrub, then where is the kb-prompt the sole machine-readable signal? The code answers precisely. A <template> element renders invisibly in a browser, so a human reading the artifact never sees the prompt — they read the body. On public serve and static share, the scrub removes the prompt outright. The one consumer that reads the prompt and nothing else is the indexer: kb_core::parser::extract pulls the template into the prompt / prompt_size_bytes fields at index time (parser.rs:274, :288), from the private, unscrubbed source.

That reframes the convention honestly: the kb-prompt's unique audience is machine ingestion of a private artifact's source — the daemon indexing it, or a tool parsing raw HTML on the loopback path. Not humans, not the public web. Its job is to give that machine a compact, structured, parseable statement of intent and provenance. Which is exactly what the ~686 freeform prompts already provide, and exactly not the "stranger executes it step by step" contract the guide advertises.

Recommendation (triage-gated — no code touched)

The evidence points away from the instinct to "make kb-prompts bigger or mandatory." Two coherent directions, either defensible:

Option A — call it what it isAccept the kb-prompt as a compact, machine-parseable provenance & intent record for the indexing path, and stop scoring it against an "execution contract" rubric that ~1.6% of the fleet meets. Update the authoring guide to describe the role authors actually use it for. Cheapest; aligns doc to reality.
Option B — scope the contract roleIf the step-by-step contract genuinely matters, it matters only for plan-category artifacts (the excluded v024 plan is the archetype — its body points to the prompt for the verify commands). Require ROLE/STEPS only there, and drop it for research/note/landscape, where the body out-transmits the prompt anyway.

What the evidence does not support: mandating prompts corpus-wide, enlarging the 8 KB budget (no file approaches it; medians are a few hundred bytes), or justifying the prompt as a hedge against body-stripping (the scrub strips the prompt).

Limits & integrity notes

  • n = 6 artifacts, one judge, one model (Opus), one machine. No error bars, no inter-rater reliability. The perfect 6/6 ranking is not independent evidence under a non-blind judge.
  • Judge non-blindness is a real, self-documented flaw — it is survivable for the large prompt≫floor gap and fatal for the small body>prompt gap, and I have scored the claims accordingly rather than around it.
  • Sample bias: all 7 artifacts are from kb-docs, and ≥4 were authored by this same Fable program — the model wrote the prompts, produced the plans, and judged them. A same-model judge rates same-model inferences as "grounded" partly because it shares them.
  • Favorable regime: all six tested features are already shipped in the tree the agents could grep, so the prompt was never tested on genuinely greenfield, unbuilt work — the case where a handoff contract would be stressed hardest.
  • Self-correction arc (matching this program's pattern — run #4 caught inflated counts, run #5 caught a wrong mechanism): here the run's own author wrote down an "insurance" headline that the adversarial pass inverted against invariant #5's actual code. The strong claim died; the census and the prompt≫metadata result are what survived.
  • Two agents lost to a spend limit (one judge, the automated adversarial phase's first attempt) — recovered from the workflow journal and re-run; 21/21 subject cells and 6/7 judges are intact.
